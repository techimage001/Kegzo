Reverse kegel: how to do it

A reverse kegel is the opposite of a squeeze. As you breathe in, the pelvic floor softens, widens and drops slightly. Training the release matters because a muscle that cannot fully relax does not work well, and many men hold too much tension down there.

In short: breathe in slowly for about 4 seconds, let the area between your sit bones soften and widen without pushing, then breathe out and let it settle. Do 5 to 10 of these slow release breaths after every set of kegels, lying down or seated.

How do you do reverse kegel?

  1. Breathe in slowly into your belly.
  2. As you breathe in, let the pelvic floor soften, widen and drop, the opposite of a squeeze.
  3. Breathe out and let it return to neutral without squeezing.

What should you feel?

A gentle opening and heaviness low down, like the moment you start to pee.

What should you avoid?

  • Pushing or straining hard
  • Squeezing on the way back
  • Holding your breath
Coach tip: A muscle that can fully relax works better. Never skip this one.

Who is reverse kegel for?

Every man should do reverse kegels, and they matter most for anyone who tends to hold tension, sits for long hours or finds squeezing easier than letting go. They come in every Kegzo session and fill most of the Relax track.

How should you breathe?

The breath does the work here. Breathe in slowly and deeply into your belly and let the pelvic floor soften and drop with it. Breathe out without squeezing and simply let it return to rest.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

  • Pushing down hard, as if straining. The drop is gentle. Think of letting go, not pushing.
  • Squeezing on the way back up. On the out-breath, do nothing. Let the area return to neutral on its own.

How do you make it harder or easier?

Make each breath longer, up to a slow count of five in and five out. Try it sitting and standing as well as lying down. There is no harder version: the aim is a softer, more complete release.

What is a reverse kegel exercise?

Your pelvic floor is a sling of muscle at the base of your pelvis. A normal kegel lifts and tightens it. A reverse kegel does the opposite: as you breathe in, the diaphragm moves down and the pelvic floor moves down with it, so you let the area lengthen, soften and widen. You are not pushing or straining. Think of it as the feeling of a deep, relaxed breath reaching all the way to the bottom of your torso.

Men often overlook reverse kegels because most advice only talks about squeezing. But a healthy pelvic floor needs both directions: a strong lift and a full release. That is why every Kegzo session pairs squeezes with release work.

Kegel vs reverse kegel: what is the difference?

MovementSqueeze and lift up and inSoften and let the area drop down
BreathUsually on the out-breathOn the in-breath
TrainsStrength, speed and staminaRelaxation, range and control

EffortActivePassive, never pushing

You need both. Squeezes without release can leave the muscle tense and tired; release without squeezes leaves it weak. Most men do best with a few reverse kegels after every set of kegels.

How to do reverse kegels: step by step for men

  1. Get comfortable. Lie on your back with knees bent and feet flat, or sit upright on a chair.
  2. Hand on belly. Rest one hand just below your belly button so you can feel your breath.
  3. Breathe in slowly through your nose for about 4 seconds. Let your belly rise into your hand.
  4. Let the pelvic floor follow. As the breath goes down, imagine the area between your sit bones softening and widening, like a hammock filling with air.
  5. Breathe out gently and let everything return to neutral without squeezing.
  6. Repeat 5 to 10 breaths. Slow and calm beats many and rushed.

The coach in the Kegzo app counts each breath with you and shows the timing on screen, which makes it much easier to keep the rhythm.

Reverse kegel positions to try

Once lying down feels natural, these positions make the release easier to feel because they open the hips:

  • Child’s pose release: kneeling, sitting back on your heels, chest towards the floor.
  • Deep squat release: a supported deep squat, breathing into the base of the pelvis.
  • Happy baby release: on your back, knees wide, holding your feet or shins.

How often should you do reverse kegels?

A simple rule: finish every kegel session with 5 to 10 reverse kegel breaths. On rest days, a minute of release breathing on its own is plenty. There is no need to hold anything; the work is in the relaxing. If you notice you clench your pelvic floor when stressed, sitting or lifting, a few release breaths during the day help too.

Signs your reverse kegels are working

  • You can clearly feel the difference between squeezing and letting go.
  • Your kegels feel cleaner and stronger, because each one starts from a fully relaxed muscle.
  • You notice less clenching when you sit for long periods or concentrate.
  • Release breathing feels calming, not effortful.

Frequently asked questions

Is a reverse kegel the same as pushing?

No. It is a gentle letting go on the in-breath, not straining or bearing down.

How often should I do reverse kegels?

In Kegzo they come in every session, and they make up most of the Relax track.

What are reverse kegels?

Reverse kegels are relaxation exercises for the pelvic floor. You breathe in and let the muscle gently soften and drop, the opposite of the squeeze and lift of a normal kegel.

Are reverse kegels good for men?

Yes. Men often hold tension in the pelvic floor without noticing. Reverse kegels teach the muscle to let go fully, which supports better control and stronger, cleaner squeezes.

Should I do kegels and reverse kegels together?

Yes. A good pattern is a set of kegels followed by a few reverse kegel breaths, so the muscle always returns to fully relaxed. Kegzo builds this into every session.

How long should a reverse kegel take?

About one slow breath: 4 seconds in while you let the pelvic floor soften, then a relaxed breath out. Five to ten breaths is a complete set.
