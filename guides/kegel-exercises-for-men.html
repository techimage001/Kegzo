Kegel exercises for men: the complete guide

By Kegzo · Last reviewed 26 November 2026

Kegel exercises for men are squeezes of the pelvic floor, the sling of muscle between your tailbone and pubic bone. You draw the muscle up and in, hold it while breathing normally, then let it go completely. Health services in the UK, US and Australia describe the same basics: find the right muscle, train both slow holds and quick squeezes, practise most days and give it a few months. Start lying down, then build up to sitting, standing and walking.

What is the pelvic floor?

The pelvic floor is a layer of muscles that stretches like a hammock across the bottom of your pelvis, from the pubic bone at the front to the tailbone at the back. In men it supports the bladder and bowel, and the muscles at the base of the penis are part of it.

It works quietly all day. It lifts slightly when you cough, laugh or pick something up, and it lets go when you go to the toilet. Like any other muscle, it can be trained. It can get stronger, react faster, keep going for longer and, just as important, relax fully when it should.

Most men never think about it, which is exactly why a little regular training can make a noticeable difference.

Why do men train their pelvic floor?

Health services such as the Mayo Clinic, the Cleveland Clinic and Continence Health Australia all recommend pelvic floor training for men, mainly to support bladder and bowel control. Men commonly train it to:

  • Cut down the after-pee dribble, the few drops that arrive after you have zipped up.
  • Stay in control when you cough, sneeze, laugh, lift or exercise.
  • Support the core, because the pelvic floor works with your deep belly and back muscles.
  • Feel more confident, with more stamina and confidence where it counts.

Kegels can help and may improve these things with regular practice, but they are not a cure for anything. If you have symptoms that worry you, speak to a GP or a pelvic floor physiotherapist.

How do you find the right muscle?

Finding the right muscle is the most important step, and many men get it wrong at first by squeezing their bum, belly or thighs instead. Try these cues lying down, with your knees bent:

  1. The wind cue: imagine you are trying to stop yourself passing wind. Feel the ring of muscle around the back passage tighten and lift.
  2. The flow cue: imagine stopping the flow of urine midway. Only imagine it. Health services advise against regularly stopping and starting your stream on the toilet.
  3. The lift cue: as you squeeze, you should feel the base of the penis draw in slightly and the testicles lift.

Put a hand on your belly and one on your bum. Both should stay soft. If either tightens, ease off and try again with less effort. For a step-by-step version, see find and lift, the first exercise in the Kegzo programme.

How do you do a kegel correctly?

  1. Lie on your back with your knees bent and feet flat. Let your belly, bum and thighs go soft.
  2. Breathe in normally.
  3. As you breathe out, draw the pelvic floor up and in, using the cues above.
  4. Hold the squeeze while you keep breathing quietly. Start with three to five seconds.
  5. Let it go completely. Feel the muscle soften and settle.
  6. Rest for as long as you held, then repeat.

The release matters as much as the squeeze. Physiotherapy guidance from the NHS stresses feeling the muscles relax and soften every time you let go. A muscle that never fully relaxes does not work well.

What are the five types of kegel exercise?

A good programme trains several qualities, not just one long squeeze. These are the five that Kegzo builds into every week:

  • Strength: firm holds of 5 to 10 seconds, such as power holds and the pyramid.
  • Speed: quick squeezes with a full release, such as quick flicks and wave squeezes.
  • Stamina: lighter holds kept for longer, such as the endurance hold.
  • Control: lifting and lowering in stages, such as the elevator.
  • Release: letting the muscle soften and drop, such as the reverse kegel.

Health services describe the same split in simpler words: slow holds for strength and stamina, and fast squeezes for quick reactions. Doing both is important.

How many kegels should a man do a day?

Guidance varies a little, but it points the same way. The Mayo Clinic suggests aiming for at least three sets of 10 a day. The US National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) suggests working up to 10 to 15 repetitions each time you exercise. Many NHS physiotherapy leaflets suggest a set of long holds and a set of quick squeezes, three times a day.

A practical starting point for most men:

  • Slow holds: 5 to 10, holding each for as long as you can keep it strong, up to 10 seconds.
  • Quick squeezes: 10, each a full squeeze and a full release.
  • How often: two or three short sets spread through the day, on most days.

More is not better. If the squeeze fades or you start holding your breath, stop the set. Quality beats quantity every time.

How should you breathe during kegels?

Keep breathing the whole time. Holding your breath raises the pressure in your belly and pushes down on the pelvic floor, which works against the squeeze. Australian government guidance for men is clear on this: do not hold your breath, only squeeze and lift, and do not tighten your bum or tummy.

A simple rhythm that suits most people: breathe in and relax, breathe out and lift, then keep breathing quietly through the hold. For the release, breathe in slowly into your belly and let the pelvic floor soften and drop. The belly breath release teaches exactly that.

Which position is best for kegels?

  • Lying down is the easiest place to start, because gravity is not working against you and the rest of your body can relax.
  • Sitting comes next. Sit tall on the front half of a chair with your feet flat. This is the position you can use at a desk.
  • Standing is harder, because the pelvic floor has to lift against gravity.
  • Walking and moving is the final step, where the pelvic floor learns to work while the rest of you is busy.

Once a position feels easy, move on to the next. The Cleveland Clinic also suggests starting seated with back support, or lying on your back or side.

How long do kegels take to work?

Continence Health Australia says many men see results within weeks of regular, correct practice. Most structured programmes run for around three months, and Australian government guidance suggests seeking help if you notice no change after three months.

Expect the first week or two to be about learning the movement. Holds usually get longer and steadier over the first month. Keep a simple record, such as your longest strong hold each week, so you can see the progress even on days when it does not feel like much is happening.

How do you fit kegels into your day?

The men who get results are the ones who keep going, so make kegels easy to remember. Link each set to something you already do every day, so the habit has a trigger:

  • Morning: a lying-down set before you get out of bed.
  • Daytime: a seated set while the kettle boils, at your desk or on your commute.
  • Evening: a standing set while you brush your teeth.

The Mayo Clinic suggests the same idea: fit a set of kegels into a routine task. A reminder on your phone helps too. Short, regular practice beats a long session once a week, and keeping a streak going is one of the simplest ways to stay motivated.

Kegels also have an everyday use beyond training. Squeezing just before you cough, sneeze, laugh or lift, and holding it until the effort is over, is a technique called the Knack. Once you can do a quick, firm squeeze on demand, it is worth practising.

How can you tell if kegels are working?

Progress with kegels is quiet, so it helps to look for specific signs rather than waiting to feel different. Common early signs include:

  • You can find the muscle straight away, in any position, without your bum or belly joining in.
  • Your longest strong hold gets longer, for example from three seconds to eight.
  • Quick squeezes feel sharper, with a clearer release between each one.
  • You notice the squeeze in everyday moments, such as standing up from a chair.

A simple weekly test makes progress visible: once a week, time your longest strong hold and count how many quick squeezes you can do in ten seconds. Write both down. The Kegzo app does this for you with a weekly max-hold test, so you can see the trend over the 28 days.

Do kegels change with age?

Men of any age can train the pelvic floor, and the basics are the same at 25 and at 75. What changes is the starting point and the pace.

  • Younger men often find the muscle quickly and progress fast. The common mistake is squeezing too hard and skipping the release.
  • Men over 50 may need a few more sessions to find the muscle clearly and may progress more steadily. Starting lying down and building up slowly works well.
  • Older men may prefer shorter holds, more rest and supported positions such as lying down or sitting with back support.

Whatever your age, go at your own pace and stop anything that hurts. If you are recovering from surgery or have a health condition, check with your doctor or physiotherapist before you start.

What are the most common kegel mistakes?

  1. Squeezing the wrong muscles. The bum, belly and inner thighs should stay soft.
  2. Holding your breath. Keep breathing quietly through every hold.
  3. Squeezing too hard. Half effort with good form beats maximum effort with help from other muscles.
  4. Skipping the release. Let go completely after every squeeze, and rest as long as you held.
  5. Doing kegels on the toilet. Stopping your stream is a cue to find the muscle, not an exercise to repeat.
  6. Giving up too soon. It takes weeks, not days. Short, regular sessions win.

Who should be careful with kegels?

Kegels are not right for everyone. If you have pelvic pain, discomfort when sitting, or a feeling that you cannot relax down there, your pelvic floor may already be too tight, and squeezing harder can make things worse. Start with release work such as the reverse kegel and speak to a GP or pelvic floor physiotherapist.

Also get professional advice if you cannot feel a clear squeeze and lift, if anything hurts, after surgery, or if you are unsure whether kegels suit you. A specialist physiotherapist can check your technique and tailor a plan.

Frequently asked questions

Can I do kegels anywhere?

Yes, once you know the feeling. Nobody can see a kegel, so seated and standing practice can happen at a desk, in a queue or on a train.

Should I feel kegels in my stomach?

No. Your belly should stay relaxed. Feeling it in your stomach usually means the abs are doing too much, so ease off and try again with less effort.

How long should I hold a kegel?

Start with three to five seconds and build up towards ten, as long as the squeeze stays strong and you keep breathing. Rest for as long as you held.

Is it OK to do kegels every day?

Yes. Short daily sessions are what most guidance suggests. If the muscle feels tired or sore, take a rest day and focus on release work.

Are kegels only for older men?

No. Men of any age can train the pelvic floor. Men with signs of a tight pelvic floor should focus on relaxation first.
